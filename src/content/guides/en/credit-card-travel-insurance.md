Premium credit cards often advertise travel insurance as a perk. It can be a good base — but the cover is defined by the bank's group contract, not by your trip, and the gaps are easy to miss.

## What card insurance usually includes

Depending on the card level:

- medical expenses abroad and assistance;
- medical repatriation;
- trip cancellation or interruption;
- delayed baggage or flights;
- travel accident cover;
- sometimes rental car excess.

## The conditions to meet

- **Payment with the card.** Most cover only applies if the trip — or a defined part of it — was paid with the card.
- **Trip length.** Cover is often limited to the first weeks of a trip abroad, commonly around 90 days.
- **Who is covered.** The cardholder, and sometimes the family, with definitions that vary.
- **Advance and claim.** You may need to pay costs yourself and claim afterwards.

## The typical gaps

1. **Medical limits** that can be modest for the United States, Canada or Japan.
2. **Excesses** that leave part of each claim at your expense.
3. **Cancellation limits** per trip or per year that are lower than the cost of a long-haul holiday.
4. **Exclusions** for adventure sports, pre-existing conditions or older travellers.
5. **No named certificate** for visas.

## How to check your card

Download the **insurance notice** from your bank and look for:

- the medical expenses limit and the repatriation cover;
- the maximum trip duration;
- the payment condition;
- the cancellation reasons and limit;
- the assistance phone number.

## When a dedicated policy makes sense

- Long-haul trips, especially to North America.
- Trips longer than the card's maximum duration.
- Expensive holidays where cancellation cover must match the trip cost.
- Travellers with pre-existing conditions or adventure activities.
- Visa applications.

If your card's medical cover is solid, a **cancellation-only** policy can fill the gap; if it is not, a full policy is the simplest solution.
